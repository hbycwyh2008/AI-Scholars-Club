# Unit 1 — AI History and Thinking Humans

**Suggested length:** 8 × 70–75 minutes  
**Core text:** Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans*  
**Current lesson bank:** [`02_Class_Missions/01_AI_History_and_Thinking_Humans`](../../../02_Class_Missions/01_AI_History_and_Thinking_Humans/README.md)

This unit comes immediately after the course introduction. Its job is to build the **AI conceptual map before students specialise in tools, datasets, or models**.

## Seminar Sequence

Use the existing eight lesson packets:

1. What counts as intelligence?
2. Neural networks, machine learning, and AI cycles
3. How machines recognise images
4. What did the model actually learn?
5. Reward, games, and reinforcement learning
6. Language processing and understanding
7. Common sense, abstraction, and analogy
8. Whole-book synthesis: how intelligent is AI?

Students read, retrieve, discuss claims, inspect evidence, and revise explanations. Do not replace the seminars with chapter summaries.

## Why This Comes First

Before students learn Pandas, scikit-learn, or a catalogue of algorithms, they should understand:

- symbolic AI versus learning-based AI;
- why neural networks disappeared and returned;
- the relationship among AI, machine learning, deep learning, CV, NLP, and RL;
- the difference between demonstrated capability and claims about intelligence;
- why different AI problems require different representations and evidence.

This gives later technical material a place in a coherent map rather than presenting algorithms as isolated tricks.

## Early Problem-Framing Warm-Ups

At this stage, keep task-recognition warm-ups light. Ask students to identify:

```text
What is the problem?
What is the input?
What output would count as success?
What evidence would convince us that the system works?
```

Do **not** require a full baseline/model/metric routine before students have learned the relevant data and ML vocabulary. The formal recurring task-recognition routine begins in Unit 2 and continues for the rest of the pathway.

## Evidence

- reading evidence and claim audits from the Mitchell packets;
- one evolving AI concept map;
- short explanations comparing symbolic AI, ML, DL, RL, CV, and NLP;
- one misconception correction per seminar;
- final synthesis: what current AI can do, what remains difficult, and what evidence supports the claim.

## Exit Gate

Student can:

- explain the major AI traditions without treating all AI as deep learning;
- place ML, neural networks, CV, NLP, and RL inside a broader AI map;
- distinguish capability evidence from anthropomorphic claims;
- explain why later technical choices depend on the problem and available evidence.
